Classic Traveller shipped in 1977, in three little black books: _Characters and Combat_, _Starships_, and _Worlds and Adventures_. It drew on the science fiction its author grew up reading: Dumarest, Flandry, Piper's Space Viking, the Dorsai, Foundation. Its future came from what a Cold War, pre-internet, pre-genomics moment could imagine. It came with almost no setting, only the tools for a referee to build one. The Third Imperium came later, in supplements and adventures. This post sets the Imperium aside and goes back to the three books: what the 1977 rules assume, read from today.

## The premise

The update is about worldview, not rules. Every edition since 1977 has reworked the mechanics. Far fewer have asked whether the universe the rules imply still makes sense to someone who carries a networked computer in their pocket, has watched machine learning arrive, knows of thousands of exoplanets, and reads empire as a problem.

The goal is the setting someone would build **today**, starting from the same sources and the same fun: small ships, long distances, thin margins, and people who matter because nobody is coming to help.

Much of what dates Traveller can't be deleted, because it holds the game up. So each concept faces one test: **what job does it do, and does the update still get that job done?** The answers fall three ways. Some concepts still do their job and still make sense, and they stay. Some read as dated, but a current version does the same job, and they change. The rest are judgment calls, where changing the concept would change what Traveller _is_.

## What to keep, and why

| Concept                                                  | Why it stays                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **No faster-than-ship communication**                    | This is the engine of the setting: local autonomy, adventure in the gaps, trade profit from knowing what the next world doesn't. It is also physically defensible, since there's no known path to faster-than-light signaling. Cut it and Traveller ends. |
| **Jump as a week of isolation, with range limits**       | Geography matters. Distance costs time, fuel, and money, and the week in jump is a natural pause for the story.                                                                                                                                           |
| **Small ships, small crews, worn machinery, debt**       | It puts the players at the center of the story and sets its scale. A ship mortgage as motive has only become more relatable since 1977.                                                                                                                   |
| **Character generation as a biography, including death** | This is the game's signature. It produces people with pasts and makes the character's survival into play an achievement.                                                                                                                                  |
| **Tech level varies world to world**                     | Uneven distribution reads truer now than in 1977.                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| **The referee builds the universe**                      | The three books ship dice and tables and leave the map blank. A subsector rolled at the table, with nothing known beyond its edge, is the strongest thing 1977 left, and it is why this update can start fresh.                                           |
| **Frontier law and local improvisation**                 | This is the play space. The center can't reach the edge in time, so people at the edge decide.                                                                                                                                                            |

## What to change, and why

| Concept                          | From (1977)                                            | To (today)                                                                                                    | Why                                                                                                                                                      |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **World generation**             | One biome, one government, one law level               | Exoplanet realism: red dwarfs, tidal locking, water worlds, rogue bodies, several regions per world           | It costs play nothing and adds texture. A world with a single biome is an old complaint about the rules.                                                 |
| **Information on a world**       | A world is uninformed until a ship arrives             | Every world runs its own networks, surveillance regime, and information ecology; the lag is _between_ worlds  | No modern society is unnetworked. The change keeps the information lag intact and makes it sharper: each world has its own consensus, and they disagree. |
| **Ecology and climate**          | Barely present                                         | A first-order fact about every world: terraforming debt, collapse, carrying capacity                          | Climate is now the frame for any story about a world, and it gives patrons real problems.                                                                |
| **Careers**                      | Military and professional services, then mustering out | Add contract, gig, corporate, and precarious lives; keep the biography mechanic                               | It reflects today's working lives. The mechanic is the part worth keeping; the 1970s career list can go.                                                 |
| **Corporate power**              | Merchants as one service among six                     | Foreground corporations alongside the nobility                                                                | It matches where power sits today, and it gives patrons an employer.                                                                                     |
| **The polity's viewpoint**       | A distant government in the background                 | Whatever polity the referee places, told with its costs visible: client worlds, absorbed cultures, extraction | Readers now see empire as a system with a story about itself. Showing its costs leaves play as it is and deepens every patron encounter.                 |
| **Identity and family defaults** | 1970s defaults                                         | Current defaults for gender, family, and personhood                                                           | It removes friction at the table.                                                                                                                        |
| **Battlefield technology**       | Slug-throwers and lasers, no robots, no drones         | Drones and autonomous systems are common; a human still makes the decision                                    | Real combat has changed. Keeping a human at the decision point is what keeps the player relevant.                                                        |

## Judgment calls that could change the nature of Traveller

Each of these could turn Traveller into a different game. For each, this post sets out what's at stake and the ways through.

### Artificial intelligence and ship computers

In 1977, computers that weigh tons and can't think are what make crews necessary. Real machine minds make pilots, navigators, and even captains optional, and player agency erodes with them.

There are several. One is a taboo of Dune's Butlerian kind; others are a historical catastrophe that left a law behind, or a hard limit, such as minds that don't survive jump. AI could instead join the crew as _characters_ with legal personhood, which makes it part of the cast instead of a threat to it. HOSTILE suggests keeping the tech retro and owning it as a style. The Distant Fringe suggests letting the computers be smart and limiting the drive instead.

### Genetic engineering and longevity

The players play recognizable humans. Anagathics, the anti-aging drugs, are already in Book 1, but routine editing and uplift would make baseline humans a choice, and maybe a minority.

Baseline humans can stay the cultural norm, with modified humans at the edges. Modification can become a marker of class and wealth. Or it can run freely, and the setting turns posthuman, which is no longer Traveller.

### Nobility

Book 1 hands out titles with high Social Standing, and patrons, intrigue, and the reason the center doesn't micromanage all hang from them. Take nobility out and much of the texture goes with it. Keep it unexamined and it reads as romance.

Nobility can be a ceremonial layer over corporate and bureaucratic power. It can stay real power, strangeness and all, as in Dune. Or Social Standing can measure wealth and prestige, as it does in Clement Sector.

### The polity as frame or antagonist

Free traders work _within_ a system. The three books barely sketch one, so the update has to pick, and the pick matters: if the setting makes its polity the villain, play becomes resistance and the game becomes a different genre.

The polity can be one distant interstellar state, shown with its costs but kept as the frame. It can be rival states, as in 2300AD. Or there can be no polity above the world at all, as in most of Clement Sector.

### Psionics

Book 3's psionics reads as a 1970s parapsychology fixture, but the rules already distrust it: talents trained in institutes that society keeps at arm's length. It can stay rare, real, and suppressed; it can be reframed as neurotechnology; or it can go.

### Aliens

The three books have no alien sophonts, only animal encounters, so adding them changes the cast. The later habit of major races in costume is where the stereotype risk lives. The galaxy can stay human, with traces of the dead, as Clement Sector keeps it. It can fill with many small native peoples, as the Distant Fringe does. Or it can gain a few great powers, the way later Traveller did, each written from inside with its own internal diversity.

### Abundance

Speculative trade requires scarcity and price gradients. Full automation and cheap fusion would erase both, and the merchant game would go with them. The way through is scarcity that stays local and uneven, an abundant core and a scarce frontier, so trade stays meaningful where the information lag is.

### Not on the table

Faster-than-ship communication isn't a judgment call. Changing it ends Traveller, and the "keep" table says why.

## What the better ATUs already decided

Alternate Traveller Universes (ATUs) do what 1977 invited: take the rules and build a universe for them. They show which concepts survive when someone rebuilds the setting from scratch on the same engine.

### Clement Sector (Independence Games, formerly Gypsy Knights)

Set in 2342. A wormhole near Epsilon Eridani opened a far region of the galaxy to colonists from Earth in 2210, then collapsed in 2331 and cut them off.

Physics keeps Clement's ships small. The Zimm drive reaches 2 parsecs at most, ships above 2,000 tons risk drive failure, and no interstellar ship exceeds 5,000 tons. That gives the small-ship universe an in-world reason, the same thing the AI call needs. Isolation works at two scales: ships still carry the news between worlds, and the wormhole's collapse cut the whole sector off from Earth: the information lag at its largest.

Clement defines the rest by what it leaves out. Instead of an empire there is one modest federation, and most worlds are independent, so government and military careers serve a specific world. Nobility is gone too; Social Standing measures wealth and prestige, a clean answer to the nobility call at the cost of the patron-and-intrigue texture. The sector is too small for megacorporations, so the corporate layer is a set of mid-sized organizations the players meet in person. Of aliens, only traces of the dead remain, which keeps a sense of deep time and sidesteps the alien-races problem entirely.

What it adds is time. Lifespans run up to 250 years, with aging starting at 76, a yes to the longevity call that changes what a character's 40-year history means. Its lifepath goes further than Classic Traveller's, through education, careers, mishaps, relationships, and aging, which confirms that the biography mechanic is what to keep.

Clement keeps every item on the keep list and makes most of the judgment calls by subtraction. It reads as current because it is smaller.

### The Distant Fringe and the Empty Stars (Adie Alegoric Stewart and contributors, Traveller Wiki)

Terran refugees flee to a cluster about 1,800 parsecs from Terra and settle it in 2255 AD. The premise grows from Third Imperium history, but its lessons don't depend on it. Humanity arrives as refugees, and the setting is about survivors.

Its Dark Age has a cause. For roughly 800 years, the Voyagers—unintelligent spacefaring organisms drawn to starships and nuclear reactions—made space travel impossible. Worlds fell to industrial tech or below, and fear of technology spread. The Fringian Renaissance is rebuilding from hidden caches, and its fear of technology has a history.

Its technology is uneven in the other direction. It tops out at TL 12 and Jump-2, but it is stronger in computers and robotics than in space technology. That inverts the 1977 profile: smart machines, short-legged ships. Crews stay necessary because the drive is limited.

Some 36 billion of the region's 127 billion sophonts belong to many small native peoples, none of them a major interstellar power. And the map is bigger than anyone knows, with lost settlements, rumored realms that may be fiction, and uncontacted natives.

The Fringe shows the AI call can be settled without dumb computers. Limit the ship, not the mind, and keep the frontier honest with a catastrophe people remember.

### 2300AD (GDW 1986; now Mongoose's in-house ATU)

Nation-states carried into space after a world war. The stutterwarp drive replaces jump, and France leads the powers. Replacing the empire with rival nations makes politics recognizable without making it allegorical, and the new drive shows that how ships move draws the map, and with it the game.

### HOSTILE (Zozer Games)

Retro-industrial frontier in the mold of _Alien_ and _Outland_: corporate exploitation, blue-collar crews, machinery rather than yachts. Travel is measured in parsecs per week instead of jumps. Its politics are modern, with corporations as the empire, but its technology stays _retro on purpose_, as an aesthetic. Dated technology works when the setting owns it as a style instead of passing it off as a forecast, which gives the AI call a fourth option.

### Orbital 2100 (Zozer Games)

Hard science fiction in our own solar system at TL 9. The Earth and the Moon are in a cold war; Mars and Mercury are independent colonies. Remove faster-than-light travel and the setting becomes a different game. Orbital 2100 is a good game, but its existence confirms that the information lag and jump are what make Traveller Traveller.

### What the ATUs agree on

Every one of them keeps small crews, dangerous travel, and lifepath character generation. Most drop or shrink the empire, none treats nobility as the main source of power, and most put corporations or nations forward as the powers players actually meet. The ones closest to Traveller constrain the drive to keep the map meaningful. Aliens are optional: Clement cuts living ones, 2300AD keeps a few, and the Distant Fringe makes them many small native peoples, and none needs a roster of major races. And where a setting caps technology, the better ones give a catastrophe as the reason, as the Fringe does with its Voyagers.

## A proposed setting from the three books

The ATUs above mostly modernize by **subtraction**. Starting from 1977 makes subtraction unnecessary, because the three books never added the empire, the races, or the history. The rest follows from a few rules. The three books are the canon: what the 1977 rules state or imply stays, and nothing published later is binding. There is one new physical fact at most, and every other update is a reading of what the rules already contain. And every dated concept keeps its job; if an update stops a concept doing its job, the update loses.

### What stays

Jump 1–6 stays, with its week in jump, refined and unrefined fuel, and no faster-than-ship communication; ships carry the news along the communication routes. So do the UWP, tech levels, starport classes, trade and speculation, and ships built by the ton, computers included. Characters still come through the six services by lifepath, with the chance of death, and Social Standing keeps its titles. Psionics stays, rare and distrusted. The referee keeps patrons, rumors, and animal encounters, and still rolls the subsector.

Everything the Third Imperium added—its history, maps, races, and megacorporations—stays out. A referee can bring any of it in; the setting doesn't need it.

### The one invention: jump-space decoherence

**High-order synthetic minds don't survive jump intact.** A machine mind coming out of jump space is degraded, unstable, or gone. Simple ship computers come through; thinking ones don't.

Everything about AI follows from that one fact. Worlds are full of AI, and ships are not: planetary economies, cities, and starports run on machine minds, and a starship is the one place where people still do the thinking. Ship computers stay heavy, because jump-hardened computing needs shielding and redundancy, so the 1977 tonnage now has an in-world reason. Crews are necessary again, since pilots, navigators, and engineers can go where the machines can't.

AIs are also local. A world's AI can't travel, so each one diverges with its world, and moving one between worlds means rebuilding it from a snapshot shipped as data, which raises identity questions for the setting's fiction. Interstellar states fear AI for good reason: minds they cannot move are minds they cannot rule, and a polity that spans worlds can govern only the space between them.

### Updates read from the three books

| **The polity's costs** | Told from the edge as often as from the center: tariffs, client worlds, navy bases, worlds absorbed as the polity grew.                                         | Naval and scout bases on the subsector map                    |
| **Corporate power**    | Corporations step forward. They are the powers most players actually work for or against.                                                                       | The Merchants service and the free trader                     |

### What it costs

The price is small. Jump-space decoherence is one invented physical fact, and every other change is a reading. Telling the polity from the edge changes no rule, but players will feel the shift in tone. And later Traveller won't drop in unchanged: supplements and adventures that assume the Third Imperium, or show thinking machines aboard ships in jump, need adapting.

### Why it's still Traveller

Every item on the "keep" list survives untouched, and the decoherence rule _strengthens_ two of them: crews matter, and the information lag now applies to minds as well as messages. A 1977 player would recognize the rules and the referee's subsector. A 2026 player would recognize the worlds.

## Open questions

Four questions stay open. Is the update a setting with its own subsectors, or a set of lenses any referee applies to a subsector rolled from Book 3? Do the three books alone set the canon, or do the next few years—_Mercenary_, _High Guard_, the early adventures—count too? Are there aliens, and if so, many small peoples or a few great powers? And is the output for play at the table, for fiction, or for an essay about how science fiction ages?

## Next steps

The work starts with the judgment calls, AI first, since most of the others follow from it. Then comes an inventory of the remaining concepts in the three books by category (technology, polity, society, biology, astrography, economy), each sorted into keep, change, or judgment call. The test is one subsector: roll it with Book 3, then write a few worlds through the new lenses.
