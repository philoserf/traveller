Before I played _Dungeons & Dragons_, there was _Afrika Korps_. In 1976 I sat down to Avalon Hill’s game of Rommel’s desert war: a mounted hex map of North Africa, a sheet of cardboard counters for the Axis and Allied units, and a rule that no attack went forward without supply.

My grandfather had been part of the Allied effort across Africa, Italy, and France, though I knew little of what he went through. He had died two years earlier, of injuries he sustained in that war. I don’t know how I found the game, but I enjoyed the challenge and the history.

More Avalon Hill games followed over the years: _Battle of the Bulge_, _Anzio_, _PanzerBlitz_, _Tobruk_, _Wooden Ships & Iron Men_, and _Squad Leader_. Later came _TAC AIR_, built to teach the air-land tactics of the Army I was serving in.

GDW’s games reached the table too. The company began as a wargame house in 1973. Its first game was _Drang Nach Osten!_, the start of the Europa series, and its first science fiction game, _Triplanetary_, appeared the same year. I played its _Harpoon_ and _Belter_, and two set in Traveller’s universe, _Mayday_ and _Snapshot_. _Third World War_ and its expansions filled a large table for weeks at a time.

Most of these games played the same way: cardboard counters on a hex map, settled with six-sided dice.

I enjoyed them all, but Traveller is the one that stuck. The wargames needed a table. Traveller I could play behind my eyes, standing in line, walking the dog, or sitting on the bus.
